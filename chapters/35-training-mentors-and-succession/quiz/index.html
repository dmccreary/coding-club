    Quiz: Training Mentors and Building a Club That Outlasts You¶

    Test your understanding of advanced mentor development, documentation and knowledge transfer, the club playbook document, and strategic planning for a club built to outlast any one leader with these review questions.

    1. What is a standard operating procedure (SOP)?¶

    1. A leader's personal judgment call about when to end an activity early
    2. The specific, formatted document -- Purpose, Materials Needed, Steps, Troubleshooting -- that documenting-procedures work produces for one recurring task, so every SOP in the club looks and reads the same way regardless of who wrote it
    3. A scored rubric used during a mentor interview
    4. A multi-year ambition for the club, such as opening a second location
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter defines an SOP as the consistent, templated document -- Purpose, Materials, Steps, Troubleshooting -- that turns a recurring task into something any mentor can follow the same way regardless of who originally wrote it. Option A describes the harder-to-write-down judgment the knowledge transfer process handles instead, not an SOP. Option C describes the mentor interview rubric, an unrelated hiring tool. Option D describes a multi-year vision plan, a different planning document entirely.

    Concept Tested: Standard Operating Procedure See: Standard Operating Procedure

    2. How does documenting club procedures differ from the knowledge transfer process?¶

    1. Documenting club procedures captures know-how that can be written as a fixed sequence of steps, while the knowledge transfer process moves harder-to-write-down judgment -- such as which parent to call first, or when to end an activity early -- through shadowing, co-leading, and structured handoff conversations
    2. They are the same process, and the chapter uses the two terms interchangeably
    3. Documenting club procedures only applies to safety rules, while knowledge transfer applies to everything else
    4. Knowledge transfer replaces the need for any written SOP
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter's own example contrasts a numbered ten-step setup SOP with judgment like "watch how three kids have already drifted toward the door" -- a signal no SOP could specify as a step, which is exactly why knowledge transfer through shadowing exists alongside written procedures. Option B ignores the chapter's explicit distinction between the two practices. Option C invents a safety-only restriction on documentation the chapter never states. Option D contradicts the chapter, which treats both practices as complementary, not substitutes.

    Concept Tested: Knowledge Transfer Process See: Knowledge Transfer Process

    3. According to the chapter's three-tier mentor certification program, what does reaching the Certified tier unlock?¶

    1. The ability to train other mentors and cover for the founder with no oversight
    2. The ability to run a session solo, after completing the curriculum, an observed session, and a peer review
    3. Only the ability to assist a Certified or Lead Mentor, never to lead independently
    4. A permanent seat on the oversight board
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's own tier table states that Certified status requires the curriculum plus an observed session plus peer feedback, and unlocks running a session solo -- exactly the mentor promoted in the chapter's own worked example. Option A describes the Lead Mentor tier's unlocked responsibility, a level beyond Certified. Option C describes the Trainee tier instead, the level below Certified. Option D invents a board-seat consequence the chapter never connects to mentor certification.

    Concept Tested: Mentor Certification Program See: Mentor Certification Program

    4. A club has a professional electrical engineer who is technically outstanding but visibly uncomfortable leading a room of ten-year-olds, and a retired teacher who knows little about wiring but commands a room effortlessly. Both are currently rotated through the same "lead the welcome circle" duty. What does the chapter say the club should do?¶

    1. Continue rotating both mentors through identical duties, since consistency matters more than individual fit
    2. Remove the engineer from the mentor roster entirely, since discomfort with public speaking disqualifies a mentor
    3. Reassign the engineer to display and robot kit prep, which plays to deep technical comfort without requiring public performance, and assign the retired teacher to leading welcome circles and transitions, producing two mentors doing their best work instead of two doing an adequate job at the wrong task
    4. Require the retired teacher to complete additional electrical safety certification before either mentor's assignment can change
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's own worked example describes exactly this reassignment, illustrating the concept of bringing out mentor strengths by matching each mentor's individual talents to the role where those talents matter most. Option A directly contradicts the chapter's explicit point that rotating everyone through identical duties produces mediocre results across the board. Option B is a disproportionate response the chapter never suggests for a mismatch that reassignment easily solves. Option D invents an unrelated certification prerequisite that has nothing to do with matching strengths to roles.

    Concept Tested: Bringing Out Mentor Strengths See: Bringing Out Mentor Strengths

    5. A departing founder narrates out loud during a co-led session, "I'm ending the robot activity ten minutes early -- watch how three kids have already drifted toward the door, that's the signal, not the clock." Which process does this illustrate, and why couldn't a written SOP have captured it?¶

    1. Documenting club procedures, since it describes a repeatable, numbered sequence of steps
    2. New leader onboarding's six-week timeline specifically, since it happens during week 3
    3. Mentor certification, since it determines which tier the departing founder holds
    4. The knowledge transfer process, since this kind of real-time judgment call only transfers by a successor watching it happen and hearing it explained out loud, not by reading a numbered list of steps
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter's own worked example uses this exact quote to illustrate the knowledge transfer process, explaining that no SOP could have specified "watch for drifting toward the door" as a numbered step -- the successor only learns it by watching and hearing it named in the room. Option A misapplies a fixed-steps process to a judgment call the chapter explicitly says resists being written as steps. Option B confuses the specific technique being demonstrated with the broader onboarding timeline it happens to occur within. Option C confuses this moment with an unrelated tier-assignment process.

    Concept Tested: Knowledge Transfer Process See: Knowledge Transfer Process

    6. Two board members independently interview the same mentor candidate using the same five fixed questions and a shared 1-3 scoring rubric, both landing on a score of 2 out of 3 for patience. Why does the chapter say this shared, specific score matters more than a vague gut feeling?¶

    1. Because a shared rubric lets two different interviewers reach the same conclusion about the same candidate, rather than one interviewer's "seemed nice" and another's "seemed distracted" being impossible to reconcile into a single decision
    2. Because a numeric score is required by the club's data privacy policy
    3. Because only rubric-based interviews can be entered into the club playbook document
    4. Because a 2-out-of-3 score automatically disqualifies a candidate from mentoring
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter explains that without a shared rubric, two interviewers' impressions of the same candidate cannot be reconciled into one decision, while a shared, specific score gives the club something concrete to act on, such as pairing this candidate with a veteran mentor first. Option B invents a data-privacy connection unrelated to interview scoring. Option C fabricates a playbook-eligibility restriction not discussed in the chapter. Option D misreads the scenario, since the chapter uses the 2-out-of-3 score to justify a supervised pairing, not a disqualification.

    Concept Tested: Mentor Interview Process See: Mentor Interview Process

    7. In the chapter's three-year club health metrics dashboard, enrollment climbs steadily from 30 to 85, but mentor retention rate drops from 70% in Year 2 to 55% in Year 3, even as average session attendance stays roughly flat. What does the chapter's own annotation say this combination reveals?¶

    1. The club is unambiguously healthy, since enrollment and attendance both look strong
    2. The data must contain a data-entry error, since enrollment and retention cannot move in opposite directions
    3. Growth in enrollment is masking a mentor problem -- the retention drop is an early warning sign a founder relying only on general impressions of "how things are going" could easily miss
    4. Waitlist length is the only metric worth tracking, since the other three are unreliable
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's own callout on this exact dashboard states plainly that enrollment keeps climbing while retention just dropped 15 points, and that growth is masking a mentor problem -- precisely the kind of decline that numbers catch and gut feeling can miss. Option A ignores the retention drop the chapter explicitly flags as a warning sign. Option B jumps to an unsupported data-error explanation the chapter's own worked scenario does not suggest. Option D overstates the case; the chapter tracks all four metrics together specifically because no single one tells the whole story.

    Concept Tested: Club Health Metrics See: Club Health Metrics

    8. How does an annual strategic plan differ from a multi-year vision plan?¶

    1. An annual strategic plan looks only one year ahead and stays concrete enough to check off at the next planning meeting, while a multi-year vision plan looks three to five years ahead at a larger ambition, such as opening a second location, that each year's annual plan takes one concrete step toward
    2. They are the same document required by the oversight board, just filed under two different names
    3. A multi-year vision plan is reviewed weekly, while an annual strategic plan is reviewed only once every five years
    4. An annual strategic plan is written by mentors, while a multi-year vision plan is written exclusively by students
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter explicitly distinguishes the two: the annual plan looks one year ahead with checkable priorities, such as recruiting four new mentors, while the multi-year vision looks three to five years ahead at a larger ambition that annual plans compound toward over time. Option B ignores the chapter's clear distinction between the two document types. Option C reverses the actual review cadence of each document. Option D invents an authorship restriction the chapter never states for either plan.

    Concept Tested: Multi Year Vision Plan See: Multi Year Vision Plan

    9. A club's board answers "yes" to all four questions on the chapter's sustainability checklist: every recurring task has a written SOP, at least one certified mentor besides the founder could run a full session solo tomorrow, the club playbook is current within the last twelve months, and the board knows exactly who the acting leader would be if the founder left this week. Evaluate this club's position relative to the single-leader-dependency problem this book opened with.¶

    1. A "yes" on all four questions is only meaningful if the founder has already left
    2. The club still depends entirely on the founder, since a checklist alone cannot change that
    3. The club needs to also pass a state-level accreditation exam before it can be considered sustainable
    4. The club has functionally solved the problem this entire book set out to solve, since the checklist is specifically designed to catch single leader dependency before it becomes a crisis
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter states directly that a club answering "yes" to all four sustainability checklist questions has functionally solved the single-leader-dependency problem the book opened with in Chapter 1. Option A misreads the checklist's purpose, which is to verify readiness before a departure, not only to confirm it after the fact. Option B contradicts the chapter's own explicit conclusion about what a full "yes" means. Option C invents an accreditation requirement that does not exist anywhere in the chapter.

    Concept Tested: Sustainability Checklist See: Sustainability Checklist

    10. Following the chapter's own six-week onboarding worked example, construct the correct order of stages for a new leader's onboarding plan, from start to full handoff.¶

    1. Run a full session solo with no one observing, then meet the venue host, then read the SOPs and playbook last
    2. Read every SOP and the club playbook, then co-lead two sessions with the outgoing founder narrating decisions, then personally meet the venue host and board treasurer, then run one full session solo while the founder observes silently, ready to step in only if something goes seriously wrong
    3. Meet the venue host and treasurer first, skip co-leading entirely, then read the playbook after the handoff is already complete
    4. Have the outgoing founder run every session indefinitely, since a new leader never actually needs to lead solo before a full handoff
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's own six-week worked example sequences exactly these stages in this order: reading SOPs and the playbook, co-leading with narrated decisions, meeting key outside contacts directly, and finally running a solo session with the founder observing silently as a safety net. Option A reverses the order, running solo before any preparation or contact-building happens. Option C skips co-leading entirely and reorders contact-meeting before any reading, contradicting the chapter's sequence. Option D contradicts the entire purpose of onboarding, which builds toward independent solo leadership, not indefinite founder dependence.
