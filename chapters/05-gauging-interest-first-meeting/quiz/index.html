    Quiz: Gauging Interest and Holding Your First Meeting¶

    Test your understanding of surveying interest, assessing feasibility, founding a club, and running a strong first meeting with these review questions.

    1. What is a gauging interest survey?¶

    1. A short questionnaire, typically five to eight questions, distributed to prospective families to measure demand for a coding club before committing resources
    2. A form parents fill out after their child's first session to rate the mentor
    3. A required government filing before a club can legally meet
    4. A long-form interview conducted only with the club's founding team
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter defines a gauging interest survey as a short questionnaire distributed to prospective families or a venue's existing patrons to measure demand before any resources are committed. Option B describes a post-session evaluation, a different kind of feedback tool. Option C invents a legal filing requirement not discussed in the chapter. Option D misdescribes the survey's audience, which targets prospective families, not the founding team.

    Concept Tested: Gauging Interest Survey See: Gauging Interest Survey

    2. What is a founding team?¶

    1. Every family that responds positively to the gauging interest survey
    2. The small group, typically two to four people, who share the initial workload of establishing the club
    3. The venue's paid staff assigned to oversee the club
    4. A panel of outside experts who approve the club's charter
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter defines a founding team as the small group of typically two to four people who split the initial workload -- securing a venue, handling paperwork, and designing the first session. Option A confuses survey respondents with the people actually organizing the club. Option C invents a staff role the chapter never assigns to founding. Option D describes an approval panel, closer to an advisory committee, not a founding team.

    Concept Tested: Founding Team See: Founding Team

    3. What is the walk-in experience?¶

    1. The process of registering online before a student's first session
    2. The full ninety-minute agenda of the first club meeting
    3. The first ninety seconds a new family spends physically entering the room
    4. The survey a family completes upon leaving their first session
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter defines the walk-in experience as the first ninety seconds a new family spends physically entering the room, shaped by deliberate first impression design. Option A describes online registration, a separate process. Option B describes the entire meeting agenda, not just the entry moment. Option D invents an exit survey the chapter does not describe.

    Concept Tested: Walk In Experience See: Walk In Experience

    4. Why does the chapter say "would your child attend a coding club every other Saturday morning at this library" produces more useful data than "would you be interested in STEM programming"?¶

    1. Because the shorter question always produces a higher response rate
    2. Because naming a specific day, time, and venue forces a respondent to check their real calendar rather than give polite agreement
    3. Because STEM programming is a term only older students understand
    4. Because the shorter question is required by most survey software
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that a vague question like "would you be interested" invites polite agreement, while a specific question forces a respondent to check their actual calendar, producing a more honest and usable answer. Option A makes an unsupported claim about response rate tied to question length. Option C invents an age-comprehension issue not discussed. Option D fabricates a software requirement irrelevant to the actual reasoning given.

    Concept Tested: Interest Survey Analysis See: Interest Survey Analysis

    5. Why does the chapter recommend counting "definitely would attend" separately from "might attend" during interest survey analysis?¶

    1. Because combining them into one "interested" total can obscure very different levels of real demand between two surveys with the same combined count
    2. Because "might attend" responses must legally be discarded before reporting results
    3. Because only "definitely would attend" responses come from families with school-aged children
    4. Because combining the two categories always overstates interest by exactly double
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter shows that a survey with 40 "might attend" and 7 "definitely" responses tells a very different story than the reverse split, even though both could be summarized as the same total, so keeping the counts separate preserves that distinction. Option B invents a legal discarding rule not in the text. Option C makes an unsupported claim about respondent demographics. Option D fabricates a fixed mathematical relationship the chapter never states.

    Concept Tested: Interest Survey Analysis See: Interest Survey Analysis

    6. Why does the chapter recommend a starting small strategy even when a survey suggests strong demand for a much larger first cohort?¶

    1. Because most venues legally cap first-time programs at three students
    2. Because a smaller group keeps the mentor-to-student ratio comfortable and gives the founding team room to discover and fix a flawed agenda before it affects many students at once
    3. Because larger cohorts always produce lower parent satisfaction scores
    4. Because a small cohort avoids the need for a gauging interest survey altogether
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that starting small keeps the mentor-to-student ratio comfortable while the founding team is still learning what works, and gives early lessons learned log entries real sessions to draw from before scaling up. Option A invents a legal cap not discussed anywhere in the chapter. Option C makes an unsupported satisfaction claim. Option D confuses starting small with skipping the survey step, an unrelated earlier stage.

    Concept Tested: Starting Small Strategy See: Starting Small Strategy

    7. A library's analyzed survey shows the 9-11 age range has both the highest total responses and the highest proportion of "definitely would attend" answers, compared to the 6-8, 12-14, and 15-18 ranges. Based on the chapter's example, what should the founding team conclude?¶

    1. All four age ranges should be recruited equally since every range showed some interest
    2. The survey should be discarded and a new one distributed only to the 9-11 age range
    3. The 9-11 age range is the strongest starting cohort, even though every age range shows some interest
    4. The 15-18 age range should be prioritized because older students need less supervision
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's own worked example concludes that the 9-11 range, having both the most responses and the highest proportion of definite interest, is the strongest starting cohort, even though every age range shows some interest. Option A ignores the concentration of interest the data actually shows. Option B discards useful evidence unnecessarily. Option D substitutes an unsupported supervision argument for the actual survey data.

    Concept Tested: Interest Survey Analysis See: Interest Survey Analysis

    8. A founding team has confirmed a venue and assembled a team of three people, but has not yet confirmed any background-checked mentor. Following the chapter's feasibility decision path, what should happen next?¶

    1. Announce a first meeting date immediately, since the venue and team are both confirmed
    2. Skip mentor screening since the founding team members already know each other
    3. Cancel the club plan entirely and restart the interest survey from scratch
    4. Complete background checks before announcing a date, since a club cannot legally run its first session without at least one screened adult present
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter's decision path is explicit: even with a venue and a founding team in place, at least one background-checked mentor must be confirmed before announcing a date, since a session cannot legally run without one. Option A skips a required feasibility pillar the chapter treats as non-negotiable. Option B wrongly assumes familiarity substitutes for a formal background check. Option C is an overreaction; the missing piece is fixable, not a reason to restart survey work.

    Concept Tested: Club Feasibility Assessment See: Club Feasibility Assessment

    9. In the chapter's sample first-meeting agenda, the 70-80 minute block is set aside for students to briefly show what they made. What is this block's stated purpose?¶

    1. To verify parental consent forms are signed before students may participate
    2. To assign students to next semester's pilot cohort
    3. To collect the gauging interest survey from attending families
    4. To build the peer-learning culture described in Chapter 1
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter's agenda table lists the 70-80 minute show-and-share block as building the peer-learning culture from Chapter 1, by having students briefly show each other what they made. Option A describes the 0-10 minute welcome and sign-in block instead. Option B is not a stated purpose of any agenda block in the chapter. Option C confuses the first meeting's agenda with the earlier interest-gauging stage, a separate step in the process.

    Concept Tested: Meeting Agenda Design See: Meeting Agenda Design

    10. During a family's first ninety seconds in the room, a parent scans for whether enough adults are present and whether someone already knows her child's name, while her child is instead checking out whether the other kids on the center table project look like they're having fun. What does this contrast best illustrate?¶

    1. Parent perception focuses on safety and organization cues, while student perception focuses on whether the room looks fun and other students seem engaged
    2. Parents and students always notice exactly the same cues during a walk-in
    3. Only parent perception matters for whether a family returns to a second session
    4. Student perception is only relevant during the guided hands-on project block, not at walk-in
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter's table draws exactly this contrast: parent perception centers on supervision and organization, while student perception centers on whether the room looks fun and other kids seem engaged. Option B contradicts the chapter's explicit distinction between the two. Option C ignores the chapter's point that both perceptions independently affect whether a family returns. Option D wrongly restricts student perception to a later agenda block, when the chapter ties it specifically to the walk-in moment.
