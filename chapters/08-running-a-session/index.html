    Running a Session: Room Layout to Cleanup¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter walks through a single session end to end: warm-up and wrap-up activities, transitions between stations, and the safety basics (exits, first aid, accessibility) every mentor should know. It closes with the less glamorous but essential routines -- check-in, photography policy, and cleanup -- that keep a session running smoothly. You will be able to run a complete session from open to close.

    Opening and Structuring the Session¶

    Before students arrive, a few setup routines make the rest of the session easier. Charging station setup means designating one spot, usually near an outlet-equipped wall, where laptops and devices charge between sessions, clearly separate from the active workspace so a charging cable never crosses a walkway. Whiteboard usage turns a simple whiteboard into the session's visible anchor -- the day's agenda, the challenge card's key steps, or a running list of "cool bugs we found," giving students something to glance at without having to ask a mentor "what are we doing now."

    Sessions run best when broken into a defined session time block structure -- distinct chunks of time, each with a clear purpose, similar to the first-meeting agenda from Chapter 5 but repeated every week rather than just once. Every session should open with a session warm-up activity: a short, low-stakes task that gets students building immediately, such as a five-minute "predict what this code does" puzzle, easing the transition from arrival chatter into focused work. It should close with a session wrap-up activity -- a brief show-and-share or a one-sentence reflection ("what's one thing you'd try differently next time") that gives the session a clear ending rather than just trailing off when time runs out.

    Within the main block, transition between activities deserves as much planning as the activities themselves; a mentor who gives a two-minute warning before switching tasks ("we're wrapping up this part in two minutes") avoids the abrupt disruption of announcing a change with no notice. Larger clubs often organize the middle of a session around small group rotation: dividing students into clusters of three or four who rotate through different short activities together, or station-based learning, where the room itself is divided into fixed stations -- a soldering station, a Scratch station, a debugging-help station -- and students move between them on a timer. Station-based learning works especially well once a club has grown past the single-project pilot cohort from Chapter 5, since it lets different skill levels work at different stations simultaneously.

    Structure is what makes 'informal' feel safe

    Here's a subtlety worth sitting with: Chapter 1 defined a coding club as informal learning, and this section is describing fairly rigid time blocks and rotations. That's not a contradiction -- predictable structure around the edges of a session is exactly what makes the middle of it feel safe enough for students to take creative risks.

    Safety, Accessibility, and Comfort Basics¶

    A handful of safety and comfort details need to be second nature to every mentor before a session begins, not looked up mid-session. Safety zone marking designates any area involving tools like soldering irons or hot glue with visible tape or a physical boundary, so a younger sibling wandering the room knows at a glance where not to touch. Emergency exit awareness means every mentor, not just the club leader, knows the room's exits and the evacuation route before the first student arrives -- a detail easy to assume someone else has covered. First aid kit location should be fixed and known to every mentor, stocked at minimum with bandages and cold packs for the occasional pinched finger or bumped head. Accessibility of space covers whether the room's layout, including the center table height and pathway widths from Chapter 7, works for a student using a wheelchair or other mobility aid -- worth checking before, not during, a family's first visit.

    Two policies round out student comfort. A restroom break policy states plainly that students may use the restroom whenever needed without asking special permission, paired with the child safety policy's supervision rules from Chapter 3 about how a student is escorted if the venue requires it. A snack and water policy clarifies whether the club provides snacks, whether students may bring their own, and how any food allergies noted on parental consent forms (Chapter 3) are handled around shared snack tables.     Don't wait for an emergency to learn the exits

    Watch out for this: a mentor who has never walked the emergency exit route or checked the first aid kit's contents is not actually ready to co-run a session, even if they've passed their background check. Walk every new mentor through the safety map above during their first onboarding session, not after something goes wrong.

    Check-In, Photography, and Cleanup¶

    Three routines bookend every session outside the main activity block. The visitor check-in process covers not just students but any parent, sibling, or guest observer entering the room -- a simple sign-in sheet at the door, distinct from the student attendance itself, that keeps the club aware of exactly who is present at any moment. Photography during session follows directly from the photo release forms in Chapter 3: only students whose families signed a photo release appear in any photo taken for the newsletter or social media described in Chapter 7, which is easiest to enforce with the colored-lanyard system mentioned there.

    Finally, the session cleanup routine closes every session the same way: kits returned to their labeled storage bins from Chapter 4, cables coiled and stored, chairs returned to their default arrangement, and the whiteboard wiped -- ideally with students themselves participating, since packing up is itself a small, repeatable responsibility that reinforces the club values from Chapter 3.

    Make cleanup part of the session, not an afterthought

    Here's a shortcut: build five minutes of cleanup directly into your session time block agenda, with students assigned specific jobs (kits, cables, chairs). A cleanup routine that's planned for takes five minutes; one that's improvised at the last minute takes fifteen and leaves everyone frustrated.

    Chapter Summary¶

    A well-run session follows a predictable shape: a warm-up activity opens it, small group rotation or station-based learning structures the middle, and a wrap-up activity closes it, with deliberate transitions between each block. Safety basics -- marked safety zones, known exits, a fixed first aid kit location, and an accessible pathway -- need to be second nature to every mentor, not looked up mid-session. Visitor check-in, a consistent photography policy tied to signed releases, and a genuine cleanup routine round out a session that runs the same reliable way every single week.

    You can run a session start to finish

    You now know how to structure a session, keep it safe and accessible, and close it out cleanly. Next up: getting the mentor-to-student ratio right so every student gets the attention they need.
