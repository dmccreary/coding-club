    Running Events and Learning From Retrospectives¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers the operational side of running events: check-in systems, attendance tracking, and post-event surveys. It places special emphasis on the event retrospective -- debriefing what worked and what didn't after every session -- as the practice that turns one good event into a repeatable one. You will be able to run a post-event survey and lead a retrospective.

    From sign-up to lessons learned

    Let's build something great -- and get better at it every single time! This chapter closes the loop Chapter 1 opened: post-event notes becoming a real, repeatable retrospective habit.

    Confirming and Reminding¶

    The moment a family registers, two automated messages keep them informed without extra manual work. A registration confirmation email goes out immediately after sign-up, restating the date, time, venue, and what to bring -- the first concrete proof to a family that their registration actually worked. A registration reminder email, sent a day or two before the session, catches the family who registered weeks ago and genuinely forgot; most registration systems, including the Eventbrite setup from Chapter 11, can automate both without a mentor lifting a finger.

    Two policies need to be settled and communicated before they're ever needed. A cancellation policy states plainly what happens if the club itself cancels a session -- weather, venue closure, insufficient mentors -- and how families are notified, ideally through the same channel as the reminder email so nobody misses it. A refund policy applies specifically to any club that charges fees (Chapter 30 covers the no-fee-by-default philosophy this book generally recommends): stating clearly whether a missed or canceled session is refunded, credited toward a future session, or neither, decided and published before the first family pays anything, not improvised after the first complaint.

    Automation earns back the time it takes to set up

    Notice the theme connecting confirmation emails, reminder emails, and Chapter 2's AI-powered tools: none of this needs a human writing individual messages every week. The upfront work of setting up automated confirmations and reminders pays for itself the very first time a session runs without anyone having to remember to send them.

    Checking In and Tracking Attendance¶

    On the actual day, a reliable session check-in system replaces guesswork about who's actually present with a clear, consistent process at the door -- whether that's a paper sign-in sheet or a digital tool. QR code check-in is a popular digital option: a printed code at the entrance that a parent or student scans with a phone to check in automatically, feeding directly into the club's attendance records without anyone re-typing names. Whichever method a club uses, the underlying attendance sheet is the actual record produced -- who attended which session -- and it feeds three separate needs at once: the mentor capacity planning from Chapter 9, any grant reporting requirements, and the visitor check-in process from Chapter 8.

    Choose the check-in method your actual mentors will use consistently

    Here's a shortcut: a fancy QR code check-in system that a mentor finds confusing under time pressure gets abandoned by week three in favor of scribbled names on a scrap of paper. Pick whichever method -- paper or digital -- your actual mentors will use reliably every single week, not the one that looks most impressive.

    Post-Event Surveys¶

    A post-event survey is a short set of questions sent to families after a session or term, distinct from the mentor and student feedback collection Chapter 10 covered informally in person -- a survey captures written, slightly more considered feedback, and reaches families who might not say much at pickup. Good survey question design keeps it short, three to five questions, and specific: "what's one thing that could be better?" produces more useful answers than "did you enjoy the session?", which nearly everyone answers yes to regardless of their actual experience.

    Survey response analysis turns those responses into action the same way interest survey analysis did in Chapter 5 -- looking for a pattern repeated across multiple respondents rather than reacting to any single comment. One family mentioning the room felt loud might be an outlier; five families independently mentioning it is a signal worth acting on, tying directly into the noise level management guidance from Chapter 7.

    The Retrospective¶

    Everything this chapter has covered feeds into the practice this book treats as central to sustainability: the retrospective. An event debriefing meeting is a short, structured conversation among mentors -- ten to fifteen minutes, ideally right after the session while memory is fresh -- covering what worked, what didn't, and what to try differently next time. Running it consistently is easier with an event retrospective checklist: a short, standing list of questions the meeting always covers (attendance versus registration, any incidents, survey highlights, one thing to change), so the debrief doesn't depend on whoever happens to remember to ask the right questions that day.

    Tracking what was learned is the step that actually closes the loop -- writing debrief conclusions into the lessons learned log from Chapter 1, not just discussing them out loud and letting them evaporate by the next session. That tracked history is also where proactive mentor engagement comes from: a club leader who reviews the log regularly can reach out to a mentor about a recurring pattern -- "I noticed the Tuesday group has been running short on time three sessions in a row, want to talk through the pacing?" -- before that mentor gets frustrated enough to bring it up themselves. The workflow below shows how one retrospective, done consistently, turns into lasting club evolution.

    A debrief that isn't written down doesn't count

    Watch out for this: a great debrief conversation that stays only in mentors' memories has the same single-leader-dependency problem as a club run entirely from one person's head. If it isn't written into the lessons learned log, it will not survive a mentor's next few busy weeks, let alone a leadership change.

    Planning Ahead¶

    Retrospective findings feed forward into how future events get planned. Session capacity planning revisits the mentor-gated math from Chapter 11 for each specific upcoming session, accounting for any mentors who've indicated they can't attend. Multi-session series planning looks further out, laying out an entire term's worth of sessions at once -- topics, dates, and mentor assignments -- so no single week gets planned in a rush the night before. A summer camp format is a distinct planning mode entirely: a compressed, often daily, multi-day intensive rather than the club's normal weekly rhythm, requiring its own capacity plan, its own registration window, and often its own dedicated group of mentors willing to commit to consecutive days rather than a single weekly session.

    Chapter Summary¶

    Automated confirmation and reminder emails, paired with clear cancellation and refund policies, keep families informed without manual effort. A consistent check-in system and attendance sheet capture who was actually present, and a well-designed post-event survey, analyzed for patterns rather than isolated comments, surfaces what's really working. All of it converges on the retrospective: a same-day debrief, run against a standing checklist, with every finding tracked in the lessons learned log -- the practice that turns a single good session into a club that keeps getting better.

    You've closed the loop on every event

    You can now run a post-event survey, lead a retrospective, and make sure every lesson actually gets tracked instead of forgotten. Next up: the special events beyond a regular session -- workshops, field trips, and showcases -- plus the payment and weather policies that support them.
