    Quiz: Building Mentor-Student Relationships That Last¶

    Test your understanding of check-in routines, feedback and attendance tracking, mentor recruitment sources, and preventing mentor burnout with these review questions.

    1. What is a mentor check-in routine?¶

    1. A quarterly written survey mentors must complete to remain active
    2. An annual performance review conducted by the club leader
    3. A short conversation or text at the start of each session asking a mentor if there is anything the leader should know before the session
    4. A formal exit interview conducted when a mentor leaves the club
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter defines a mentor check-in routine as a two-minute conversation or a quick text the night before, asking "anything I should know before today," which catches a no-show risk or a rough week before it becomes a surprise. Option A confuses this with mentor feedback collection, a separate periodic process. Option B invents a formal performance review the chapter never describes. Option D describes an exit interview, unrelated to the routine, ongoing check-in described.

    Concept Tested: Mentor Check In Routine See: Mentor Check In Routine

    2. What is a substitute mentor plan?¶

    1. A curriculum plan used only when a professional mentor is unavailable
    2. A rule requiring every session to have exactly one substitute present at all times
    3. A list of parent volunteers who supervise field trips
    4. Naming, in advance, who covers for a mentor who cancels last-minute, so a single absence never drops the session below a safe ratio
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter defines a substitute mentor plan as naming in advance who covers for a last-minute cancellation, so the ratio stays safe even with one absence -- a form of redundancy applied at the single-session level. Option A misapplies the term to curriculum planning, an unrelated concept. Option B invents a rule requiring a substitute at every session, which contradicts the plan's actual as-needed purpose. Option C confuses it with an unrelated field-trip supervision list.

    Concept Tested: Substitute Mentor Plan See: Substitute Mentor Plan

    3. What is mentor-student rapport?¶

    1. The accumulated trust and familiarity that makes a student comfortable asking a "dumb" question or admitting they're stuck
    2. A formal written agreement signed by both a mentor and a student
    3. The technical skill level a mentor has achieved in a specific programming language
    4. The number of sessions a mentor has attended in total
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter defines mentor-student rapport as accumulated trust and familiarity built through many small moments, making a student comfortable admitting confusion rather than hiding it. Option B invents a formal written agreement the chapter never describes. Option C confuses rapport with technical skill, an unrelated mentor attribute. Option D confuses rapport with attendance tracking, a separate concept entirely.

    Concept Tested: Mentor Student Rapport See: Mentor Student Rapport

    4. Why does the chapter identify mentor pairing consistency as the steadiest predictor of a strong mentor-student relationship?¶

    1. Because it guarantees a student will never be paired with a peer mentor
    2. Because consistency lets trust build cumulatively instead of resetting every time a student meets a different mentor
    3. Because it is required by the club's liability insurance policy
    4. Because it eliminates the need for any student check-in routine
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that the same mentor working with roughly the same small group week after week lets trust accumulate rather than reset with every new pairing. Option A invents a restriction on peer mentor pairing not discussed. Option C fabricates an insurance requirement unrelated to pairing consistency. Option D wrongly claims consistency replaces check-ins, which the chapter treats as a separate, still-necessary routine.

    Concept Tested: Mentor Pairing Consistency See: Mentor Pairing Consistency

    5. Why does the chapter recommend collecting student feedback through informal conversation rather than a written form?¶

    1. Because written forms are prohibited by the club's data privacy policy
    2. Because younger students rarely fill out surveys thoughtfully, so a question like "what's your favorite part of club" at pickup captures more honest signal
    3. Because only mentor feedback collection may legally use a written form
    4. Because informal conversations take less time to analyze than survey responses
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that younger students rarely complete surveys thoughtfully, so an informal question at pickup captures more honest signal than a written questionnaire. Option A invents a data-privacy prohibition not discussed. Option C fabricates a legal distinction between mentor and student feedback methods. Option D focuses on analysis time, a reason the chapter never gives.

    Concept Tested: Student Feedback Collection See: Student Feedback Collection

    6. According to the chapter, what is often the first visible sign of mentor burnout, appearing before a mentor actually misses a session?¶

    1. A mentor filing a formal written complaint
    2. A mentor requesting a raise in volunteer stipend
    3. A mentor asking to switch from small group mentoring to whole class instruction
    4. A normally enthusiastic mentor going quiet about session ideas
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter's warning states that a mentor who used to chat enthusiastically and has gone quiet is often the first visible sign of burnout, well before they actually miss a session. Option A invents a formal complaint process not mentioned as a burnout indicator. Option B is irrelevant since most mentors are unpaid volunteers, not stipend-based. Option C invents an unrelated teaching-format request as a burnout signal.

    Concept Tested: Mentor Burnout Prevention See: Mentor Burnout Prevention

    7. A club needs a mentor available on weekday afternoons with deep technical or teaching experience, but its working parents can rarely commit to that time slot. Which mentor source does the chapter suggest is best suited to this specific need?¶

    1. College mentor recruitment, tied to the academic semester
    2. Teen mentor program, recruited through local high schools
    3. Retired professional mentor, recruited through a senior center or a former employer's retiree network
    4. Parent volunteer role, recruited from families already dropping off students
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter states a retired professional mentor brings deep experience and often far more flexible weekday availability than a working parent, reached through a senior center or retiree network. Option A describes college mentors, whose availability ties to the academic semester rather than steady weekday time. Option B describes teens, whose own school schedules limit weekday afternoon availability. Option D describes parent volunteers, who share the same weekday-availability constraint the scenario describes.

    Concept Tested: Retired Professional Mentor See: Retired Professional Mentor

    8. A club's roster lists twelve mentors, but attendance tracking shows only five mentors and eighteen students actually present on a given night. What ratio does the chapter say this represents, and why does it matter?¶

    1. A 12:1 ratio, based on the full roster count
    2. A 3:1 ratio, exactly matching the recommended target
    3. A 3.6:1 ratio, worth noting even though the roster on paper looks fine
    4. A 1.5:1 ratio, since eighteen divided by twelve is 1.5
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's own example states that five mentors and eighteen students present tonight is a 3.6:1 ratio worth noting, even if the roster on paper looks fine, since attendance tracking reveals the club's real operating capacity. Option A wrongly uses the full roster of twelve rather than the five mentors actually present. Option B misapplies the target ratio without doing the actual calculation from the scenario's numbers. Option D inverts the calculation, dividing students by mentors incorrectly.

    Concept Tested: Mentor Attendance Tracking See: Mentor Attendance Tracking

    9. A mentor has missed three sessions in a row without giving any notice beforehand. According to the chapter's no-show policy guidance, what should the club leader do?¶

    1. Immediately remove the mentor from the club without any conversation
    2. Have a friendly but direct conversation with the mentor, since the policy responds to a pattern of no-shows rather than a single instance
    3. Publicly announce the mentor's absences during the next session's whole class instruction
    4. Say nothing, since a single no-show is never worth addressing
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter states that for a mentor, a no-show policy usually means a friendly but direct conversation after a pattern, not a single instance, of no-shows -- exactly the repeated absence described here. Option A is a disproportionate response the chapter does not recommend as a first step. Option C would embarrass the mentor publicly, which the chapter's private, direct-conversation approach does not support. Option D contradicts the chapter's guidance to address a genuine pattern once it emerges.

    Concept Tested: No Show Policy See: No Show Policy

    10. A mentor spends most of a session working one-on-one with a single student who needs focused support, briefly pauses to introduce a new challenge card to the whole room, then returns to small clusters of three students each for the rest of the session. Why does the chapter caution that the whole-class portion should stay brief?¶

    1. Because whole class instruction is the format furthest from the hands-on, individualized coaching the book otherwise emphasizes, even though it efficiently delivers the same information to everyone at once
    2. Because whole class instruction is not allowed under the club's code of conduct
    3. Because one-on-one mentoring should always take up the majority of every session
    4. Because small group mentoring can only happen after whole class instruction ends
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter describes whole class instruction as efficient for delivering the same information to everyone but the format furthest from the hands-on, individualized coaching the book otherwise emphasizes, which is why it should stay brief. Option B invents a code-of-conduct prohibition not discussed anywhere in the chapter. Option C overstates one-on-one mentoring's role, which the chapter frames as ideal for specific students, not the default for a whole session. Option D invents a sequencing rule the chapter never states.

    Concept Tested: Whole Class Instruction See: Whole Class Instruction
