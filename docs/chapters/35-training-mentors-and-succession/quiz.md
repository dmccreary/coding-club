#### 2. How does documenting club procedures differ from the knowledge transfer process?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Documenting club procedures captures know-how that can be written as a fixed sequence of steps, while the knowledge transfer process moves harder-to-write-down judgment -- such as which parent to call first, or when to end an activity early -- through shadowing, co-leading, and structured handoff conversations
2. They are the same process, and the chapter uses the two terms interchangeably
3. Documenting club procedures only applies to safety rules, while knowledge transfer applies to everything else
4. Knowledge transfer replaces the need for any written SOP
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter's own example contrasts a numbered ten-step setup SOP with judgment like "watch how three kids have already drifted toward the door" -- a signal no SOP could specify as a step, which is exactly why knowledge transfer through shadowing exists alongside written procedures. Option B ignores the chapter's explicit distinction between the two practices. Option C invents a safety-only restriction on documentation the chapter never states. Option D contradicts the chapter, which treats both practices as complementary, not substitutes.

    **Concept Tested:** Knowledge Transfer Process
    **See:** [Knowledge Transfer Process](../../glossary.md#knowledge-transfer-process)

3. Reassign the engineer to display and robot kit prep, which plays to deep technical comfort without requiring public performance, and assign the retired teacher to leading welcome circles and transitions, producing two mentors doing their best work instead of two doing an adequate job at the wrong task

</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Option D invents an unrelated certification prerequisite that has nothing to do with matching strengths to roles.

Option A misapplies a fixed-steps process to a judgment call the chapter explicitly says resists being written as steps. Option B confuses the specific technique being demonstrated with the broader onboarding timeline it happens to occur within. Option C confuses this moment with an unrelated tier-assignment process.

</div>

    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter explains that without a shared rubric, two interviewers' impressions of the same candidate cannot be reconciled into one decision, while a shared, specific score gives the club something concrete to act on, such as pairing this candidate with a veteran mentor first. Option B invents a data-privacy connection unrelated to interview scoring. Option C fabricates a playbook-eligibility restriction not discussed in the chapter. Option D misreads the scenario, since the chapter uses the 2-out-of-3 score to justify a supervised pairing, not a disqualification.
