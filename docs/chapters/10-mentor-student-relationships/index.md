## Check-In and Feedback Routines

A simple **mentor check-in routine** -- a two-minute conversation at the start of each session asking "anything I should know before today," or a quick text the night before -- keeps a club leader aware of a mentor's availability and state of mind before a session begins, catching a no-show risk or a mentor having a rough week before it becomes a surprise. The parallel **student check-in routine** happens at the door: a mentor greeting a student by name and asking one quick question ("how was your week?") before diving into the project, which does double duty as both a warmth signal and an informal read on whether a student seems off that day.

Collecting feedback deliberately, rather than relying on impression alone, closes the loop on both relationships. **Mentor feedback collection** is a short, periodic check-in -- a quarterly survey or a casual conversation -- asking mentors what's working and what's frustrating them, feeding directly into the lessons learned log from Chapter 1. **Student feedback collection** asks the same question from the other side, often more effectively through an informal conversation than a form, since younger students rarely fill out surveys thoughtfully; a mentor simply asking "what's your favorite part of club so far, and what's your least favorite" at pickup captures more honest signal than a written questionnaire ever would.

## Where to Find More Mentors

Chapter 9 named three mentor types; this section covers where to actually recruit each one. The **parent volunteer role** is often a club's most overlooked mentor source -- a parent already dropping off their own child has a built-in reason to be in the room, and many are willing to background-check and train once asked directly rather than waiting to be recruited. A **teen mentor program** formalizes peer mentoring by recruiting local high schoolers, often ones who are club alumni themselves, and can double as a service-learning or leadership opportunity a teen can list on a college application. **College mentor recruitment** reaches computer science or education students at a nearby college, frequently through a professor willing to announce the opportunity or a campus volunteering office, and tends to bring enthusiastic, technically strong mentors with a semester's worth of predictable, if not permanent, availability. A **retired professional mentor** brings decades of technical or teaching experience and, often, far more flexible weekday availability than a working parent -- a demographic frequently reached through a local senior center or a former employer's retiree network rather than through channels aimed at younger volunteers.

The table below summarizes where to look for each source.

| Mentor Source | Where to Recruit | Typical Strength | Typical Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parent Volunteer Role | Families already dropping off students | Built-in reason to be present | May need more up-front encouragement to formally volunteer |
| Teen Mentor Program | Local high schools, club alumni | Strong peer rapport with younger students | Needs closer mentor pairing consistency and supervision |
| College Mentor Recruitment | Nearby college CS or education departments | Technical strength, high enthusiasm | Availability tied to the academic semester |

## Keeping Mentors Healthy

Recruiting mentors is only half the job -- keeping them is the other half. **Mentor burnout prevention** means watching for the early signs of volunteer fatigue -- a normally reliable mentor missing sessions, seeming short-tempered, or mentioning they're "stretched thin" -- and proactively reducing their load before they quit altogether, often by simply asking "would every-other-week work better for you right now?" rather than waiting for a resignation. **Mentor appreciation** is the deliberate, regular practice of thanking mentors specifically and publicly -- a shoutout in the newsletter from Chapter 7, a small end-of-term gift, or simply the club leader personally naming what a mentor did well that session -- since volunteers who feel genuinely valued stay far longer than those who quietly assume nobody noticed their effort.

!!! mascot-warning "A quiet mentor might be a burned-out mentor"
    ![Circuit warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Watch out for this: a mentor who used to chat enthusiastically about session ideas and has gone quiet isn't necessarily fine -- that's often the first visible sign of burnout, well before they miss a session. Check in directly rather than assuming silence means everything's okay.

## Attendance and Reliability

Two parallel tracking habits keep a club's actual operating capacity visible. **Student attendance tracking** records who showed up to each session, feeding both the mentor capacity planning from Chapter 9 and any grant-reporting requirements Chapter 30 will cover. **Mentor attendance tracking** does the same for volunteers, and the two together let a club leader spot a real-time ratio problem -- five mentors and eighteen students present tonight is a 3.6:1 ratio worth noting, even if the roster on paper looks fine.

A clear **no-show policy** sets expectations for what happens when a mentor or student doesn't show up without notice -- for a mentor, this usually means a friendly but direct conversation after a pattern (not a single instance) of no-shows; for a student, it might simply mean checking in with the family to make sure everything's okay. A **substitute mentor plan** names, in advance, who covers for a mentor who cancels last-minute, so a single absence never drops the session below a safe ratio -- exactly the redundancy leadership succession (Chapter 3) is built to provide, applied at the level of a single session rather than the whole club.

!!! mascot-tip "Keep a standing substitute list, not a scramble"
    ![Circuit giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Here's a shortcut: ask two or three reliable mentors ahead of time if they're willing to be on a standing substitute list, and text that whole group the moment someone cancels. A pre-approved list gets a same-day yes far faster than messaging mentors one at a time.

## Building Real Rapport

The steadiest predictor of a strong mentor-student relationship is **mentor pairing consistency**: the same mentor working with roughly the same small group of students week after week, rather than a different mentor rotating through every session. Consistency lets trust build cumulatively instead of resetting every time. Three distinct teaching formats support that consistency differently. **One-on-one mentoring** gives a single student a mentor's full attention, ideal for a student who needs focused support but expensive in mentor time. **Small group mentoring** -- the 3:1 default from Chapter 9 -- balances personal attention against mentor capacity. **Whole class instruction**, where one mentor briefly addresses every student at once (introducing a new challenge card, for instance), is efficient for delivering the same information to everyone but should stay brief, since it's the format furthest from the hands-on, individualized coaching this book otherwise emphasizes.

All of this exists to build **mentor-student rapport**: the accumulated trust and familiarity that makes a student comfortable asking a "dumb" question or admitting they're stuck. Rapport isn't built through any single grand gesture -- it's built through **building trust with students** consistently over many small moments: remembering a detail from last week's conversation, following through on a promise to look something up, staying calm when a project breaks. The ultimate goal of all of it is **student confidence building** -- watching a student who once needed constant reassurance start attempting a new challenge card without asking permission first, which is often the clearest sign a mentor-student relationship is genuinely working.
