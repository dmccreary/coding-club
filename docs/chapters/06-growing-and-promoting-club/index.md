## Crafting Your Pitch

An **elevator pitch** is a thirty-second spoken summary of what your club does and why it matters, named for the idea that you should be able to deliver it in the time it takes an elevator to reach its floor. A strong elevator pitch answers three things fast: who the club serves, what students actually do at a session, and why it's different from a typical class. One founder's pitch to a prospective venue host ran: "We run a free, drop-in coding club for kids 9 to 14 at the library every other Saturday. Nobody sits through a lecture -- every student builds something with code or electronics and takes home a finished project. It's the only program in town where a kid can light up an LED strip they programmed themselves." That pitch got her a room booking on the spot.

Behind every good elevator pitch sits a clearer, written **value proposition statement**: a one-to-two sentence articulation of the specific benefit a family gets by joining, distinct from a mission statement (Chapter 2) because it's written to persuade an outside audience rather than to guide internal decisions. Where a mission statement might read "we give students hands-on experience with code and electronics," a value proposition sharpens that into "your child leaves every session having built something real, not just watched a demonstration" -- language aimed squarely at what a skeptical parent actually wants to hear.

!!! mascot-thinking "A pitch is a value proposition, spoken out loud"
    ![Circuit thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Notice the relationship here: your value proposition statement is the idea, written down and refined in private. Your elevator pitch is that same idea, rehearsed until it comes out naturally in conversation. Write the statement first; the pitch gets easier once the idea itself is sharp.

## Growing and Scaling

Not every club should grow, and a deliberate **club growth strategy** starts by asking whether growth is actually the goal, rather than assuming bigger is automatically better. A club that thrives at fifteen students with a tight-knit mentor team may lose exactly what made it work if it doubles in size without also doubling its mentor base. **Scaling a club** specifically means growing the existing single site -- more students, more sessions per week, more mentors -- without adding a second physical location, and it is almost always the right first move before considering anything more ambitious.

**Multi-site expansion** means running the same club model at more than one venue -- a second library branch, or a nearby school -- typically once the founding site has proven stable for at least a year and has a trained assistant leader (Chapter 3) capable of running it without the original founder present. A **franchise model** goes further still: packaging the club's charter, curriculum, and branding so that an entirely different founding team, at a location the original founder has no direct involvement with, can replicate it using a shared playbook. Very few clubs need to consider a franchise model, but the option exists for a club whose format has proven successful enough that other communities are asking how to copy it. ## Brand and Onboarding

**Club branding** extends the naming and logo choices from Chapter 4 into a consistent visual and verbal identity across every flyer, newsletter, and social post -- the same colors, the same tone of voice, the same logo placement, so that a family recognizes club communication at a glance rather than having to read closely to confirm it's legitimate. A consistent brand also makes the value proposition statement do more work over time: families start to associate the brand itself with "the place where kids build things," even before reading the specific message.

That brand identity shows up immediately in **welcome statement design**: the short, warm message every new family receives, whether spoken at the door or printed on a first-session handout, that sets expectations before the session even begins. A welcome statement typically covers what to expect (hands-on, drop-in, no grades), where to find restrooms and exits, and who to ask if something is unclear. **Onboarding new students** extends that welcome into the first few sessions specifically: pairing a brand-new student with a returning student for peer support (Chapter 1), and giving them a slightly simplified version of that session's project so their first experience ends in a completed build rather than a frustrating half-finished one. **Onboarding new mentors** is the parallel process for adult volunteers -- walking a new mentor through the code of conduct and child safety policy (Chapter 3), pairing them with an experienced mentor for their first two sessions, and giving them a copy of the club policies handbook (Chapter 4) before they're expected to run anything solo.

!!! mascot-warning "A great brand can't cover for a rough onboarding"
    ![Circuit warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Watch out for this: a polished logo and welcome statement create expectations that a confusing, unstructured first few sessions will immediately undercut. Make sure onboarding new students and onboarding new mentors both live up to whatever your branding promises.

## Reaching New Families

Chapter 5 covered flyers and social media for a single first meeting; this section covers the ongoing channels a growing club relies on. **Word of mouth referral** remains the highest-converting channel long after the first meeting -- a family that heard about the club from another family they already trust arrives with far less hesitation than one responding to a flyer. **Community outreach** means the club proactively reaching beyond its existing family base: a table at a local community fair, a demonstration at a town event, or a presentation at a PTA meeting.

Two specific outreach relationships deserve deliberate cultivation. **School partnership outreach** means building a relationship with a school's administration or a specific teacher who can mention the club to families directly, often the single highest-leverage relationship a community-based club can build, since a teacher's endorsement carries built-in trust. **Library partnership outreach** works similarly for library-hosted or library-adjacent clubs, where a children's librarian who personally recommends the club to families checking out books reaches exactly the families most likely to attend.

## Media and Milestone Events

As a club matures, **local media coverage** -- a segment on a local news program, a mention in a community newspaper -- can reach families no direct outreach channel touches, though it usually requires the club to initiate contact rather than waiting to be discovered. **Press release writing** is the practical tool for that outreach: a short, factual announcement following a standard format (headline, dateline, a quote from the club leader, contact information) sent to local outlets, most effective when tied to a specific newsworthy moment rather than sent as a generic "we exist" announcement.

The two most reliable newsworthy moments are milestone events. A **grand opening event** marks a new club's or new site's official launch with extra visibility -- refreshments, a demonstration table, an invitation to local media and elected officials -- deliberately bigger than a normal first meeting specifically because it exists to generate outside attention, not just to run a good session. A **club anniversary event** celebrates an ongoing club's first, fifth, or tenth year with returning alumni, a showcase of past projects, and often a second wave of local media interest -- both events work because they give outreach efforts and press releases something concrete and dateable to attach to.
