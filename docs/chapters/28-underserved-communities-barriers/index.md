## Recruiting a Club That Reflects Your Community

### Recruiting Minorities

**Recruiting minorities** means intentionally extending a club's invitation into the community spaces where Black, Hispanic, Native American, and other historically under-represented students and families already gather -- a church bulletin, a youth program, a barbershop or salon with a community board -- instead of relying only on whatever channel reached last year's group. The concept isn't about meeting a quota; it's about recognizing that word-of-mouth and a single flyer will always reproduce the network a club already has. A club run entirely on the existing mentor network's contacts will recruit students who resemble that network, no matter how welcoming the room turns out to be once they arrive.

A worked example: a club meeting in a suburban library posts flyers only in the library lobby and the PTA email newsletter. After two semesters, the roster closely matches the PTA list's demographics. The next term, the mentor adds three channels reaching outside that list: a flyer at a local Boys & Girls Club afternoon program, an announcement shared through a Spanish-speaking congregation's bulletin, and a flyer posted at a Black-owned barbershop that already sponsors a youth mentoring night. The curriculum, ratio, and schedule stay identical -- only the invitation travels further. The following semester's sign-ups include several families who had never heard of the club, because for the first time the flyer reached a space they already trusted.

### Recruiting Girls And Women

**Recruiting girls and women** addresses a specific and well-documented gap: computing clubs that promote themselves with images of boys building robots, or that frame the activity as competitive and combative, quietly signal to girls that the space wasn't built for them before they ever walk in. Fixing this gap doesn't require a separate girls-only club, though some communities benefit from one -- it requires promotional materials, activity framing, and mentor pairing that make the invitation visibly, not just technically, open to everyone.

A worked example: a club's flyer shows a single photo of two boys pointing at a robot, captioned "Build. Compete. Win." A local Girl Scout troop leader mentions that several of her scouts assumed the club was "for boys who already know coding." The mentor swaps the photo for one showing a mixed group of students -- including girls -- mid-conversation over a half-built circuit, and changes the caption to "Build something that's yours." The mentor also asks two returning girl students to help welcome new sign-ups on the first night, since a familiar face at the door does more than any flyer wording. The next session's sign-up sheet includes five girls, compared to one the prior term -- the technical content of the club never changed, only who could picture themselves in it.

### Underserved Community Outreach

**Underserved community outreach** is the broader practice of identifying which populations near a specific club location are least likely to hear about it at all -- often families with limited English, families without steady internet access, or neighborhoods a club's existing volunteer network rarely touches -- and building a deliberate plan to reach them. It goes beyond recruiting minorities and recruiting girls and women as the only two outreach targets: every community has its own specific under-reached population, and the fix always starts with naming that population specifically rather than pursuing "outreach" in the abstract.

A worked example: a club forming inside a community center serves a neighborhood with a large refugee resettlement population, most of whom rely on a resettlement agency's caseworkers for information about local programs rather than a school newsletter. A mentor who never contacts that agency will never reach those families, no matter how good the flyer is. Instead, the mentor meets with a caseworker directly, explains the club in plain terms, and leaves paper flyers at the agency's intake desk rather than posting them only online. Within a month, three students from resettled families join -- not because the flyer's wording changed, but because it finally reached the specific channel those families actually use.

A club's outreach plan is probably missing someone if:

- Every flyer has been posted in only one language.
- Every sign-up traces back to a single school, church, or mentor's personal contact list.
- No registration or reminder has ever needed to reach a family without steady home internet.
- The promotional photos show only one gender or one racial group.
- No local community organization -- library, resettlement agency, Boys & Girls Club -- has ever been asked to help share the flyer.

### Language Accessibility

**Language accessibility** means making sure a family who speaks a language other than English at home can understand what the club is, when it meets, what it costs, and how to sign up -- without needing a fluent English-speaking relative to translate every update. It covers more than translated flyers: it includes whether a mentor or volunteer can greet a family in their home language at the door, and whether registration forms and reminder texts are available in more than one language.

A worked example: a club's registration confirmation arrives only in English, and a parent who reads Vietnamese comfortably but not English cannot confirm the meeting time without asking a bilingual neighbor each week. The club adds a second-language version of just the essential logistics -- day, time, cost (none), and a phone number for questions -- sent alongside the English version, and recruits one bilingual volunteer who checks in by text rather than email, since that parent found texting easier to manage than a long message. The student's attendance, which had been inconsistent, becomes reliable once the parent can independently track the schedule.

### Translated Materials

**Translated materials** are the specific documents -- flyers, registration forms, permission slips, a one-page "what to expect" sheet -- rendered accurately into the languages spoken in a club's community, distinct from language accessibility as a broader goal. A translation only counts as accessible if a fluent speaker checks it, rather than trusting a single pass through a machine-translation tool, since automated tools frequently mangle idioms, technical terms like "sensor," and culturally specific phrasing.

A worked example: a mentor runs a permission slip through a free translation tool and hands out the Spanish version without review. A bilingual parent volunteer later points out that the tool translated "no fee required" in a way that actually read closer to "fee not guaranteed" -- the opposite of the intended meaning -- and several families who read it that way never turned in the slip. After the mistake is caught, the club adopts a simple rule: every translated document gets a five-minute read-through by a fluent speaker before it goes home with a single student, no exceptions. The cost is a few minutes of a volunteer's time; the alternative cost was families opting out over a translation error nobody meant to make.

!!! mascot-tip "Never trust a single machine translation without a human check"
    ![Circuit giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Here's a shortcut that saves real trouble: run every translated flyer or form past a fluent speaker for five minutes before it goes home with a student. One mistranslated phrase -- like "fee not guaranteed" instead of "no fee required" -- can undo weeks of outreach in a single sentence.

## Funding a Club Families Can Actually Afford

Reaching a wider community only matters if, once families arrive, cost doesn't quietly send them back out the door. [Chapter 30](../30-budgeting-fundraising-grants/index.md) covers the mechanics of building a budget and writing grants; this section covers the policy decision that makes a no-fee club worth funding in the first place, and the day-to-day supports that keep the promise real.

### Low Income Family Support

**Low income family support** covers the specific accommodations a club makes so that a family's income never determines whether their student can participate -- not just waiving a registration fee, but making sure a family isn't quietly priced out by a supply list, a fundraiser expectation, or a "suggested donation" that functions as a fee in every way except its name. Support here means auditing every cost a family might encounter, not only the headline registration price.

A worked example: a club charges no registration fee but asks each family to bring a $15 supply kit for the first project. A mentor notices two students stopped attending right after that request went out, and a follow-up conversation reveals both families simply couldn't spare the $15 that week. The club switches to buying supply kits in bulk from its own budget and treats the per-kit cost as a club expense funded through the fundraising work Chapter 30 describes, rather than a pass-through cost to families. Attendance for both students resumes the following week. The lesson generalizes: a cost doesn't have to be labeled "registration" to function as one -- any required out-of-pocket expense can silently exclude the same families a no-fee policy was meant to include.

### No Fee Club Model

**The no-fee club model** is the policy decision that a club charges students nothing to attend -- no registration fee, no per-session cost, no supply fee passed to families -- funding itself instead through grants, donations, and partnerships. Adopting the model is a one-sentence policy; sustaining it is a year-round funding operation, which is why the no-fee commitment and the funding plan that supports it have to be solved together, not one after the other.

A worked example: a new club leader announces a no-fee policy on the very first flyer, before identifying a single funding source. By the third month, laptop-repair costs and supply expenses have outpaced the leader's personal contributions, and the leader considers quietly adding a $10 fee mid-semester. Families who joined specifically because the club was free feel misled, and two drop out. The lesson: the no-fee model has to be committed to alongside a realistic funding plan -- a grant, a local business sponsor, or a parent-run fundraiser -- so the policy that recruits low-income families in the first place doesn't collapse under its own promise.

Here is how the two funding models compare on who they reach and what they demand of a club leader:

| Aspect | Fee-Based Model | No-Fee Model |
|---|---|---|
| Who joins | Families who can afford the fee, self-selected | Any interested family, regardless of income |
| Funding source | Family payments | Grants, donations, partnerships (Chapter 30) |
| Leader's year-round work | Collecting and tracking payments | Writing grants, cultivating donors, tracking in-kind support |
| Risk if underfunded | Rare -- fees cover costs directly | High -- a funding gap can force a mid-year fee or a program cut |

### Why No Fees Matters

**Why no fees matters** is the reasoning behind the policy, not the policy itself: a fee of even $20 doesn't just remove families who genuinely can't pay it -- it also removes families who could technically afford it but read the fee as a signal that the club isn't really meant for them, especially in communities where other enrichment programs have historically priced them out. The fee's dollar amount matters less than what it silently communicates about who the invitation is for.

A worked example: two nearly identical clubs open six months apart in the same town -- one charges a modest $15 fee "to keep things serious," the other is free and says so on every flyer. The free club's first-session turnout is nearly triple the fee-based club's, even though both offer the same curriculum, the same mentor ratio, and meet in similarly resourced buildings. A survey of families who skipped the fee-based club shows several assumed a fee meant equipment, prior experience, or a competitive selection process was expected -- none of which was true, but the fee implied it. Removing the fee didn't just remove a cost; it removed an assumption that was quietly doing more of the excluding than the $15 itself.

### Scholarship Fund

**A scholarship fund** is a pool of money a club sets aside -- through grants, donations, or a fundraiser -- specifically to cover costs a no-fee model doesn't fully eliminate, such as a laptop a family can't provide, transportation, or a materials fee for an advanced elective track. Even a fully no-fee club can encounter costs it can't absorb into its base budget, and a scholarship fund is the mechanism for covering those without singling out which family needed the help.

A worked example: a club is entirely free to attend, but an advanced robotics elective requires a $40 sensor kit per student that the base budget doesn't cover. Rather than asking families to pay, or excluding students who can't, the club sets up a small scholarship fund from a local business sponsor and quietly covers the kit cost for anyone who requests it -- using a single checkbox on the standard sign-up form ("kits are available at no cost, just check this box") rather than a separate application that would require a family to publicly identify as needing help. Three students check the box in the first term, and none of their peers ever know which three, because the request looks identical to not needing one.

!!! mascot-warning "A scholarship fund that requires a family to ask for it in public will go unused"
    ![Circuit warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Watch out for this trap: making a family fill out a separate "financial need" form, or ask a mentor in front of other parents, adds a shame cost that keeps families from using a fund they genuinely qualify for. Build the request into the standard sign-up form as a single checkbox everyone sees, so asking for help looks identical to not needing it.

### Free Device Loan Program

**A free device loan program** lends a club-owned laptop or tablet to a student who doesn't have reliable access to one at home, checked out for the semester the way a school issues a textbook, and returned at the end of the term. It directly supports the no-fee model's promise, since a student who has to share one family phone for homework and club projects alike faces a real barrier that no fee waiver removes on its own.

A worked example: a club purchases five Chromebooks specifically for loan rather than in-room use, using the configuration steps from [Chapter 22](../22-purchasing-configuring-devices/index.md) -- a student login, a saved-work folder, and a class bookmark folder already set up before the laptop ever leaves the building. A student who previously did all club work in-session because there was no computer at home checks one out for the semester and immediately starts finishing projects between sessions instead of only during them. The club tracks each loaned device on a simple sign-out sheet noting the student's name, the device's serial number, and the checkout date, so a five-laptop loan pool doesn't quietly become five laptops nobody can locate by the second semester.

!!! mascot-tip "Track loaned devices the same way you'll track kits later in this book"

    Here's a shortcut: log every loaned device on a simple sign-out sheet -- student name, device serial number, checkout date -- before it leaves the building. Skip this step and a device loan program usually loses track of two or three laptops by the second semester, right when the next student who needs one is asking.

## Removing Physical and Scheduling Barriers

Even a free, well-promoted club can lose students to a barrier that has nothing to do with cost or outreach: getting to the building, the meeting time, or a physical space that doesn't fit a student's needs. These barriers are just as structural, and just as fixable, as the ones already covered.

### Transportation Barrier Solution

**A transportation barrier solution** addresses the simple fact that a family without a reliable car, or with only one vehicle shared across a household's schedule, may want a student to attend and still be unable to get them there -- a barrier that has nothing to do with interest, cost, or the quality of a club's promotional materials. Solutions range from low-cost to resource-intensive: a carpool network among current families, partnering with an existing after-school bus route, providing public transit passes, or offering a hybrid virtual-attendance option for a session a student genuinely cannot reach in person.

A worked example: a mentor notices that a student who attended enthusiastically for a month suddenly stops coming, and a phone call reveals the parent's work schedule changed and they can no longer drive the 20-minute route to the library where the club meets. The mentor connects the family with another club parent who lives two streets away and already drives past the library, and the two families set up an informal carpool for the rest of the semester. No club budget, grant, or new policy was needed -- just a mentor treating the missed sessions as a solvable logistics problem rather than a sign the student had lost interest.

### Accessible Session Timing

**Accessible session timing** means choosing meeting days and times that fit the widest range of family schedules a community actually has, rather than defaulting to whatever slot is most convenient for the mentors. A weekday-after-school club excludes any student whose parent works until evening and can't arrange a ride home right after school; a Saturday-only club excludes families with weekend jobs, religious observance that conflicts with a Saturday slot, or a custody schedule that only includes weekdays. Neither timing choice is universally correct -- the right one depends on which schedule conflict is most common in a specific community.

A worked example: a club meets every Saturday morning, and attendance is solid for months until a mentor notices three students have quietly stopped coming. A quick survey reveals all three now have Saturday shifts at a family business or a part-time job that started once they turned 16. The club adds a second, smaller weekday-after-school session run by a different mentor, and two of the three students switch to it and resume attending regularly. The fix wasn't to abandon the Saturday session -- it still serves families for whom weekday afternoons don't work -- but to recognize that a single fixed time will always lose some students to a schedule conflict the club can predict and plan around.

Scheduling options worth testing in a single community, side by side rather than as an either/or choice:

- Alternating Saturday sessions, once every two weeks, for families balancing weekend jobs against a lighter time commitment.
- A weekday-after-school session for families without weekend transportation or with weekend work conflicts.
- A short virtual check-in option for a student who can't reach either in-person slot during a specific week.

### Disability Accommodation

**Disability accommodation** means adjusting a club's physical space, materials, or session pace so a student with a physical, sensory, or learning disability can participate as fully as any other student -- a ramp and an accessible restroom for a student using a wheelchair, large-print or high-contrast challenge cards for a student with low vision, noise-reducing headphones or a quieter corner for a student with sensory sensitivity, or simply more time on a task for a student who processes instructions more slowly. Accommodation is specific to the student in front of a mentor, not a single checklist that treats every disability the same way.

A worked example: a student who is hard of hearing joins a club that runs sessions in a large, echoing room where a mentor's spoken instructions are difficult to follow over background noise from other groups. Rather than assuming the student will simply keep up or fall behind, the mentor asks the student directly what would help, and the answer is straightforward: written instructions on an index card alongside the spoken version, and a seat near the front rather than in the back corner. Both changes cost nothing and take five minutes to set up each session. The mentor also checks the venue's accessible entrance and confirms it stays unlocked during club hours, since a technically compliant ramp that's chained shut during the exact hours a club meets accommodates no one.

!!! mascot-encourage "You don't need to be a disability-law expert to accommodate one student well"
    ![Circuit encouraging](../../img/mascot/encouraging.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    If the range of possible accommodations feels overwhelming, that's normal -- most mentors aren't trained in accessibility law, and they don't need to be. Start with one direct question to the student or family in front of you ("what would make this easier?"), and build the fix from their answer instead of trying to anticipate every possible need in advance.

## Chapter Summary

Reaching a genuinely representative club roster starts with recruiting minorities and recruiting girls and women through channels that travel beyond a mentor's existing network, backed by underserved community outreach that names a specific under-reached population rather than treating diversity as one generic goal, and made real through language accessibility and carefully checked translated materials. None of that outreach holds up if cost quietly filters families back out: low income family support, a genuine no-fee club model, and an honest accounting of why no fees matters all depend on the scholarship fund and free device loan program that fund the promise rather than just declare it -- work that Chapter 30's budgeting and grant-writing content makes possible. Even a free, well-promoted club can still lose students to a transportation barrier, an inflexible session time, or a disability accommodation nobody thought to ask about, which is why each of those barriers is treated here as a solvable logistics problem, not a reason a student didn't show up.
