# Quiz: Mentors, Students, and the 3:1 Ratio

Test your understanding of mentor and student roles, the three kinds of mentors, why the 3:1 ratio works, and how to add and group mentors and students responsibly with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What is a peer mentor?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A software professional who volunteers occasional guest sessions
2. An older student, typically a teenager, who mentors younger students and often explains concepts in kid-to-kid language
3. Any adult who has completed a background check
4. A parent who only attends sessions to observe, not to teach
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter defines a peer mentor as an older student, typically a teenager, who mentors younger students -- less technically experienced than an adult mentor but often better at explaining concepts the way another kid would understand. Option A describes a professional mentor instead. Option C describes the general screening requirement for adult mentors, not the peer mentor's defining trait. Option D describes an observing parent, unrelated to any mentor type.

    **Concept Tested:** Peer Mentor
    **See:** [Peer Mentor](../../glossary.md#peer-mentor)

</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter defines the student to mentor ratio simply as the number of students each mentor is responsible for during a session, with 3:1 as the recommended target. Option A describes a retention metric, unrelated to supervision ratios. Option B describes mentor tenure, not a student-supervision measure. Option D describes a mentor-type mix, a different comparison than the student-to-mentor ratio.

#### 3. What is mentor capacity planning?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Designing the curriculum tracks a professional mentor teaches

</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter defines mentor capacity planning as calculating, at any point in time, how many students the club can responsibly serve given its current mentor count, recalculated whenever a mentor joins or leaves. #### 4. Why does the chapter say ratios looser than 3:1, such as eight students per mentor, tend to fail students?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because a mentor stretched across many students spends most of the session triaging who needs help most urgently, leaving little time for patient, question-first coaching
2. Because state regulations cap coding clubs at exactly 3:1
3. Because looser ratios always cost more in liability insurance
4. Because students at looser ratios are statistically more likely to quit within a month
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter explains that at looser ratios a mentor manages only a minute or two of real attention per student per hour, spending most of the time triaging rather than coaching. Option B invents a regulatory cap the chapter never mentions. Option C fabricates an insurance-cost claim not discussed. Option D makes an unsupported statistical claim the chapter does not present.

    **Concept Tested:** Why Three To One Works
    **See:** [Why Three To One Works](../../glossary.md#why-three-to-one-works)

#### 5. Why does the chapter warn that a roster of twelve mentors doesn't guarantee twelve mentors at any given session?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because mentor availability tracking may show only some of those mentors typically attend, so real capacity should be planned against who actually shows up, not the full roster
2. Because a roster automatically expires after six months
3. Because only professional mentors are counted toward the official roster
4. Because peer mentors are never included on the roster count
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter's warning is explicit: if availability tracking shows only seven of twelve mentors typically attend, real capacity is seven mentors times three, not twelve times three. Option B invents a roster expiration rule not in the text. Option C fabricates a restriction on which mentor types count toward the roster. Option D similarly invents an exclusion rule the chapter never states.

#### 8. A club only starts recruiting new mentors after it is already over-enrolled, and spends several weeks running well above 3:1 while background checks process. What does the chapter say the club should have done differently?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Practiced adding mentors ahead of demand, since recruiting and background-checking new volunteers takes time and reacting after over-enrollment leaves the club running above ratio for weeks
2. Waited even longer before recruiting, since demand often resolves itself
3. Skipped background checks temporarily to add mentors faster
4. Closed enrollment permanently rather than recruiting more mentors
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter states that adding mentors should happen ahead of demand, not in reaction to it, since a club that waits until over-enrolled will run above 3:1 for weeks while checks process. Option B contradicts the chapter's explicit recommendation to recruit proactively. Option C violates the non-negotiable background check requirement from Chapter 3. Option D is a more drastic step than the chapter recommends, which favors proactive recruiting over closing enrollment.

    **Concept Tested:** Adding Mentors
    **See:** [Adding Mentors](../../glossary.md#adding-mentors)

#### 10. A mentor notices that in a sibling pair, the older sibling has been quietly finishing the younger sibling's coding steps for her rather than letting her attempt them herself. According to the chapter, what standard should the mentor apply?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

3. Ignore it, since sibling pairing is exempt from peer-learning standards
4. Ask the parent to sit in on every future session to supervise the pair directly
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter states a mentor watching a sibling pair should apply the same "ask questions, don't take over" standard as any peer-learning pairing, rather than letting a more skilled sibling simply do the work. Option A is a more extreme response than the chapter recommends, which favors coaching over separation. Option C directly contradicts the chapter's statement that the same standard applies. Option D invents a parent-supervision requirement not discussed in the chapter.
